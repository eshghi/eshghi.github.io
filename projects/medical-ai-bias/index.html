Bias & Fairness in Medical AI

A structured chapter on how data, algorithms, evaluation practices, and clinical systems can introduce bias into medical AI. Case-based analysis connects each source of unfairness with its practical consequences and possible mitigation strategies.

Abstract

Bias in medical artificial intelligence can enter at several stages between collecting clinical data and using a model in practice. A high aggregate accuracy can conceal unequal performance across patient groups, and an apparently technical error can reflect choices made in data collection, labeling, evaluation, or clinical workflow. This chapter examines these sources of bias to explain how they affect trust, fairness, and equity in medical AI. Its objective is to connect the origin of a bias with its practical consequences and with strategies that can reduce its effect.

The chapter organizes the problem into four domains: data-related biases, modeling and algorithmic biases, evaluation biases, and human and systemic biases. For each domain, the discussion defines the relevant concepts, considers their manifestation in medical applications, and uses case-based analysis to connect methodological choices with consequences for patients and practitioners. The organization follows the full pathway from the data available for learning to the conditions in which predictions are interpreted and acted upon. It therefore treats fairness as a property of the wider clinical system as well as of the fitted algorithm.

The resulting synthesis shows why one intervention cannot address every source of unfairness. Improving representation in training data addresses a different problem from choosing appropriate evaluation populations or managing human reliance on automated recommendations. Mitigation must be selected according to the mechanism producing the bias and assessed in the intended setting. The chapter contributes a structured account of these mechanisms and their corresponding responses, providing a basis for more transparent development and evaluation. Its main conclusion is that trustworthy medical AI depends on identifying bias throughout the lifecycle and making its effects visible, so that technical performance, equitable treatment, and clinical usefulness can be considered together.
