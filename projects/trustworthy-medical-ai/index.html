Trustworthy Medical AI

A chapter on moving medical AI from controlled experiments into clinical practice through risk-based evaluation, explainability, local validation, monitoring, and human oversight. The focus is the evidence and workflow required for dependable adoption.

Abstract

This chapter examines the future outlook of artificial intelligence in medicine and emphasizes that the successful transition of models from controlled laboratory environments to the real clinical world requires a paradigm shift from a sole focus on computational accuracy toward the development of “trustworthy artificial intelligence”. The practical application of this technology should be carried out through a risk-oriented and staged approach, so that its deployment begins with low-risk and supportive tasks and, through rigorous evaluations, expands toward sensitive decision-support systems. Although large language models (LLMs) and self-supervised learning approaches have high potential to transform the healthcare system their safe use requires domain specialization, bias control, and the use of multidimensional evaluation criteria beyond traditional performance assessment metrics. In this regard, explainable systems (XAI) should also move beyond the generation of superficial heat maps and progress toward providing reasoned, causal, and clinically aligned explanations consistent with medical guidelines. In addition, successful implementation in hospitals faces challenges such as heterogeneity of electronic health records, infrastructural limitations, and the phenomenon of data distribution shift, or Data Drift, which necessitates local validation, assessment of clinical value, and continuous post-deployment monitoring. Finally, this study argues that the future of artificial intelligence in healthcare does not mean complete automation and replacement of healthcare staff, but rather is based on the design of hybrid workflows and structured synergy between humans and machines. In this interactive model, intelligent systems play the role of clinical collaborators, while the physician will continue to be responsible for ethical judgment, contextual understanding of the patient, and final decision-making in order to achieve more precise and more equitable care.
