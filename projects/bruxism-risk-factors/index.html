Explainable Bruxism Analysis

An explainable tabular-learning study of factors associated with self-reported bruxism. Model comparison is combined with correlation, permutation importance, SHAP, statistical tests, and decision rules to examine a multifactorial outcome.

Abstract

Bruxism, commonly described as tooth grinding or jaw clenching, is a clinically important behavior because it can be linked with tooth wear, temporomandibular symptoms, orofacial pain, disturbed sleep, and other oral-health consequences. The aim of this study was not only to design a predictive classifier, but mainly to identify and interpret the risk factors associated with self-reported grinding teeth. A cross-sectional tabular dataset was analyzed. The original dataset contained 576 records. Edited Nearest Neighbours (ENN) was first used to identify and remove noisy majority-class samples, leading to 525 records. Because the class ratio between non-grinding and grinding teeth was about 8.21:1, SMOTEENN was applied only within the training folds to reduce imbalance, and the final resampled training set contained 736 samples. Ten machine-learning models were compared, including tree-based, boosting, kernel-based, instance-based, linear, and neural approaches. Hyperparameters and decision thresholds were selected through grid search optimized by F1-score. The best overall F1-score was obtained by LightGBM (F1-score = 0.821), followed by MLP (F1-score = 0.816). However, the main value of the study comes from the explainability analysis. Across Spearman correlation, model-native importance, mutual information, permutation importance, SHAP, univariate significance, decision rules, and sample-difficulty analysis, the most consistent risk-factor groups were psychological distress variables, especially anxiety, depression, and stress; insomnia and sleep-related measures, especially ISI, nightmare, sleep timing, and sleep duration; demographic and context variables such as age, roommate, and residence; and lifestyle-related variables such as digital-device exposure, tea or coffee timing, and BMI. These findings suggest that self-reported bruxism in this dataset is multifactorial and heterogeneous. The proposed approach can support screening and knowledge extraction.
