Local Logistic Classification

A divide-and-conquer classifier that partitions heterogeneous data with K-means++ and fits local logistic models only in mixed clusters. Experiments on five datasets evaluate whether local specialization improves prediction while retaining interpretable decision rules.

Abstract

Logistic Regression is known as a fundamental tool in classification data and has strong use in many different fields such as finance and healthcare due to its computational efficiency and high interpretability, yet its reliance on a global linear decision boundary often results in suboptimal performance when applied to complex, non-linear, and heterogeneous data structures. To overcome this limitation without resorting to opaque "black-box" models, this research introduces a hybrid "cluster-then-predict" framework that integrates K-means++ clustering with local Logistic Regression. The proposed methodology use a divide-and-conquer strategy, initially partitioning the input space into distinct clusters using K-means++ to isolate local data patterns, where the resulting clusters are categorized based on their purity: pure clusters are directly assigned a deterministic class label , while impure clusters containing mixed class instances are individually modeled using dedicated local Logistic Regression classifiers to resolve complex decision boundaries. Experimental evaluations conducted on five real-world datasets demonstrate the proposed method's superiority, achieving notable results such as an Accuracy of 0.98 and F1-score of 0.95 for the Give Me Some Credit dataset, and an F1-score of 0.99 for Australian Credit Approval. Comparative analysis indicates that this hybrid approach yields significantly better performance than global Logistic Regression and other similar works, confirming its effectiveness in handling data heterogeneity while preserving interpretability.

The central modeling decision is to use cluster purity to determine how much classification machinery is needed in each region. Regions containing one class can be handled directly, while heterogeneous regions retain a probabilistic local classifier. This creates a collection of simpler decision rules adapted to different parts of the feature space. The experimental comparison evaluates the complete pipeline against a single global model, connecting predictive performance with the geometric structure of the data. The results indicate that local specialization can recover useful nonlinear behavior while retaining the familiar coefficients and probabilities of logistic regression. The contribution therefore lies in the organization of the learning problem as well as in the classifier itself, making data heterogeneity an explicit part of model design.
