Tire Press Failure Prediction

A predictive-maintenance study that converts estimated intervals between tire-press faults into actionable time-window warnings. Real operational reports, label cleaning, simulation-based evaluation, and a decision-support interface connect the model to maintenance planning.

Abstract

Unexpected failures in tire curing presses interrupt production, increase waste, and raise maintenance costs in tire manufacturing. This study develops a data-driven predictive maintenance framework for forecasting Failure to Engage Timer faults in curing presses at Kavir Tire Company. Real operational data were integrated from curing defect reports, stoppage records, and performance reports. After removing unreliable records, standardizing fault labels through string-similarity-based fuzzy matching, and engineering production-related workload indicators, the final modeling dataset was used to reformulate failure prediction as an operational time-window decision problem. Instead of predicting the exact next failure date, a regression model first estimates the interval between consecutive timer engagement failures; its output is then converted into a binary warning by comparing the predicted interval with the elapsed time since the previous failure and a selected prediction window. Several regression algorithms were evaluated using a simulation-based strategy that reflects real maintenance decision points between failures. The results show that a 7-day window provides a practical balance between predictive performance and planning usefulness. For this window, Linear Regression achieved the best performance, with an F1-score of 70.09%. The proposed framework was implemented as a decision-support toolbox to help maintenance personnel plan preventive actions for curing press equipment.

The time-window formulation links the statistical model to the decision that maintenance personnel need to make: whether a fault is likely within an actionable planning period. Data preparation addresses inconsistent labels and differences among the source reports before the interval model is fitted. Simulation then evaluates warnings at decision points occurring between recorded failures, providing a closer connection to maintenance use than a comparison of interval errors alone. The study compares several candidate window sizes to examine the balance between early warning and prediction quality. Its main contribution is the combination of operational data integration, a window-based evaluation design, and a usable interface for turning estimated fault intervals into maintenance information.
