COVID-19 Mortality Modeling

A retrospective mortality-modeling study using 31,277 PCR-confirmed COVID-19 records from South Khorasan. Tree-based models and SHAP connect predictive performance with the direction and consistency of important clinical variables.

Abstract

Background In the wake of COVID-19 disease as an infected pandemic, necessitated the development of precise prognostic tools to assist clinical decision-making. This retrospective study, conducted at a referral center in South Khorasan, Iran, aimed to evaluate the utility of machine learning (ML) algorithms in predicting mortality among COVID-19 patients. Methods Using a comprehensive dataset of 31,277 PCR-confirmed cases, this study compared the predictive performance of Decision Tree, Random Forest, and XGBoost models based on demographic, clinical, and laboratory parameters. Results Findings revealed significant statistical associations between mortality and key variables, including age, sex, and clinical presentation. All three models reached a strong consensus in identifying intubation status, age, and PO₂ ​levels as the primary determinants of clinical outcomes. Further analysis using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) quantified these contributions, elucidating the directional influence of each predictor on mortality risk. Decision Tree visualization confirmed that intubation serves as the primary prognostic node, with secondary stratification occurring via consciousness levels and PO₂​ status. Conclusion These results demonstrate that ML-based risk stratification can effectively integrate complex clinical data to provide objective prognostic insights. By successfully identifying high-risk cohorts, these robust models offer a valuable framework for optimizing triage protocols and clinical resource allocation in resource-constrained, high-pressure public health environments. Clinical trial registration number Not applicable.

The analytical workflow connects model comparison with a clinical interpretation of the fitted predictors. Tree-based importance identifies variables repeatedly selected by the models, while SHAP adds information about how different variable values influence individual predictions. The decision-tree representation makes combinations of predictors visible as a sequence of explicit rules. Agreement across these views strengthens the interpretation of the variables that consistently organize the observed outcomes. The study consequently contributes both a comparative mortality-modeling analysis and a set of interpretable associations in a large regional cohort. Because the records are retrospective, these associations describe the population and clinical measurements used in the analysis. Their value lies in informing further assessment of risk stratification and in identifying which available variables deserve particular attention when developing context-specific prognostic tools.
